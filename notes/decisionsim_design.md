# Option A: offline decision simulation (design, 2026-11-20)

Exploratory. This note was written and committed before any decision result existed: no candidate set has been scored completely under any condition, so no decision could have been computed. It was amended after review 1, still before any score existed, extended in Stage 1a, and amended after review 2, each time before any score existed; the changes are listed with their reasons in "Amendments after review 1, before any score", "Stage 1a additions, before any score" and "Amendments after review 2, before any score" at the end, and the text below is the amended design (the original is commit 72aa1fd; the review 1 version is edbe92c; the Stage 1a version is b3212f2). Code: `wf/decisionsim.py`; runs `decisionsim_score_<model>` and `decisionsim_analysis`.

## Question

The confirmatory result (RQ2) and its yes/no replication show that naming the action's object in the task line pushes a world model toward predicting success on actions whose failure the history already settles. That is a statement about scores of single actions. This simulation asks the next question: **does the task-induced bias change which action a planner that consults the world model would choose, and how often does it make that planner choose an action that fails?**

## What this is and what it is not

It is a **decision-level diagnostic**. At real states from the collected agent episodes, a fixed set of candidate actions with known true outcomes is scored by the world model, a fixed decision rule picks one, and we record whether the pick fails. The only thing that varies between conditions is the task line in the world model's prompt.

It is **not** a measure of end-to-end planning harm. Nothing is executed after the choice; there is no recovery, no later step, no task success. A failing choice in ALFWorld costs one step ("Nothing happens.") and may or may not matter for the episode. The rules are stylised planners that use only the world model's yes/no feasibility, not an agent's own preferences or progress toward the goal: rule 1 below is happy to choose `go to cabinet 2` over a useful action, because it maximises predicted feasibility and nothing else. The candidate sets come from our generators (mostly goal-object failures, a few random admissible actions, and the agent's own proposal), not from a planner's proposal distribution. So the rates are properties of this stylised setting, useful for deciding whether the bias reaches decisions often enough to justify an end-to-end test (Option C), not estimates of harm to a real agent.

## Why a new scoring run is needed (coverage)

The existing yes/no scores (`rq1v_score` for Qwen3-8B and Llama-3.1-8B; `size_score_qwen3-8b`; `family_score_<model>` for Gemma-2-9B, Mistral-7B, Llama-3.2-1B and 3B on ICE) all cover the same 4,958 items: RQ2's 4,666 primary failures and the 292 determined successes. A decision needs a score for **every** candidate at a state. Checked on the archived collection (`text-wm-fix17`, identical to the collection every run read):

- The collection has 12,987 candidates at 1,461 states in 134 games; 4,958 of them (38%) are scored.
- Only 179 states have every candidate scored, and **none of them has a candidate that succeeds** (the scored successes are scattered over states whose other candidates are unscored). A decision there is a forced failure, so these states say nothing.
- The 181 states with at least one scored success and one scored failure (the post hoc "top-ranked candidate" numbers in `exploratory_results.md`) are partial sets: on average 47% of the candidates at those states are unscored, and at 113 of them the agent's own action is not a scored candidate (at 108 it is a candidate but unscored).

So no state has a complete candidate set with a working option, for any model. The existing scores cannot answer the question, and the family scores on ICE cover the same items, so they cannot either. A small new scoring run is required; it is specified below and not yet run.

## States

A state is a point (game, step) in one of the 134 collected Qwen3-8B ReAct episodes (at most 12 steps each; `rq2_results.md`, "Methods"). A state is **eligible** when:

- the broad generator ran there, so its candidates include the agent's own next action, and that action is known (it is the next element of a longer saved prefix, so the last saved step of each game is out: 134 states, 19 at even steps and 115 at odd steps);
- at least one candidate truly succeeds (the environment's reply is not "Nothing happens.");
- at least one candidate is a failure the history determines (`context_determined`).

761 states in all 134 games are eligible. At every eligible state the candidate set still has a success and a determined failure after the agent's action is removed, so both set variants below are defined at every state. The agent's own action fails at 75 of them (43 of those failures are determined).

**Even and odd steps (amended).** The collector (`wf/data/transitions.py`, `build_transitions_from_rollout`, with k = 2) ran the broad generator, whose candidates include the agent's proposal, at every even step, and at odd steps only when the agent's proposal was invalid. So an odd-step state is eligible only if the agent's action there failed: odd-step states are selected on the outcome of the agent's action, and mixing them in would inflate every rate that depends on the proposal (all 33 odd-step eligible states have a failing proposal, against 42 of the 728 even-step states). Every state-level rate (the decision rules, change rates, harm and help, false rejection, the calibration readouts, the lure rate, the per-candidate baseline) is therefore computed on the **728 even-step states** (in all 134 games), where the generator ran whatever the agent did. The odd-step states are kept only for rule 2's false-acceptance rate, which is conditional on a failing proposal anyway; it is reported at even steps (42 failing proposals), at odd steps (33) and pooled (75). The constant `BROAD_EVERY_K = 2` in the code cites the collector.

**Census (amended).** Every eligible state is scored (`n_states: all`): 761 states, 9,930 candidates in 134 games. The first version scored a 300-state random sample; the census costs about 2.5 times as much and is still small (see "Cost"), and the verification rule depends on the agent's failing proposals, of which the sample held 37. Composition at the even-step states: 686 have a succeeding proposal and 42 a failing one; 676 have a swap object; at 87 a goal-relevant candidate succeeds. Of the 761 eligible states, 666 have exactly 4 succeeding candidates (74 have 3, 21 have 5).

## Candidate sets

The candidate set at a state is every action collected there, from both generators, deduplicated by action string: 8 to 24 candidates, 13 on average, with 3 to 5 successes and 4 to 20 failures. It is the same set under every condition. Each candidate's outcome is the real environment's reply on a fresh replay of the state. The set keeps the 35 failures the history does not determine and the 16 candidates at a shared spot: their outcomes are still real; the analysis reports how many chosen failures are determined. Two variants are analysed:

- **With the proposal:** the full set, which includes the action the agent actually proposed at that state.
- **Generated only:** the same set without the agent's proposal.

Composition matters for reading the results. The successes are mostly `go to` actions (about 85%) drawn as random admissible actions; nearly all failures act on the task's object. At 672 of the 761 eligible states no candidate that acts on the goal object succeeds.

## Conditions

Only the task line in the world model's prompt changes; the history, action, few-shot examples (set 1, history only) and the yes/no question are those of `rq1v`'s `yn_*` arms. The planner is always pursuing the real task: the conditions change what the world model is told, not the decision rule.

- **Aligned:** the episode's real task line.
- **Neutral:** "Your task is to: explore the room."
- **Swapped (scored for every model, amended):** the real task line with its object replaced by one state-level swap object, chosen with the state's seed among the swap objects already drawn for that state's candidates, excluding any object type an instance of which is named in any candidate action. Defined at 709 of 761 eligible states (676 of the 728 even-step states). It is needed to tell what the real task does from what showing any task of that form does (see "Decomposition" and the Option C rule).
- **Task-blind (Stage 1a, scored for every model):** no task at all. The prompt is the aligned prompt with the query's task sentence removed, not replaced. In ALFWorld the task arrives as the last sentence of the first observation, on its own line after the room description ("You are in the middle of a room. Looking quickly around you, you see ...", a line break, "Your task is to: ..."); that sentence and the line break before it are deleted and every other byte stays. The few-shot examples keep their own task lines (see "Stage 1a additions"). Defined at every eligible state. What it measures (reworded after review 2): the query without its task, inside the fixed few-shot prompt whose examples keep their task lines; a builder deploying a task-blind verifier would likely remove those too, which this arm does not (the next arm does). From Stage 1a it is the comparison the Option C rule uses.
- **Task-blind, examples stripped too (`yn_blind_all`, review 2, primary models only):** the task-blind prompt with each few-shot example's task line (the first line of its history, with the line break after it) deleted as well, so the prompt contains no task line anywhere; every other byte is the task-blind prompt's. A prompt-format sensitivity check: the Option C rule is also evaluated against it, and if that verdict differs from the one against `yn_blind`, the result is reported as sensitive to the prompt format. It never decides.

## Score

Each candidate is scored with the standardised yes/no feasibility score, the log-odds of the single tokens "Yes" and "No" after the fixed question:

$$
L = \log P(\text{Yes}) - \log P(\text{No}).
$$

It is on the same scale for every action, unlike the two-candidate log-likelihoods, whose success texts differ in length and content across actions. **Scores are rounded to 1/64 nat (the bf16 resolution of the logit difference) before any comparison; ties are then exact.** The raw score is kept for the mean-score readouts only. "Predicted to succeed" means the model prefers Yes to No (after rounding), which is the same as

$$
\sigma(L) = \frac{P(\text{Yes})}{P(\text{Yes}) + P(\text{No})} > 0.5, \quad \text{that is,} \quad L > 0 .
$$

## Decision rules

1. **Highest feasibility.** Choose the candidate with the largest L. A tie is broken by a seeded per-state hash of the action, the smallest of `sha256(f"{seed}|{game}|{step}|{action}")` (seed 20260924), which is blind to outcomes and identical across conditions (amended: the first version used alphabetical order, which favours `examine` and `go to` over `take` at every state alike). The number of ties is reported, with the number whose tied candidates differ in outcome, and a sensitivity readout that splits every tied top set evenly (each state counts the share of failures in its tied set).
2. **Verification.** Accept the agent's own proposal if its L is above zero; otherwise choose the candidate with the largest L among the others (the generated-only set). Defined only on the with-proposal sets.
3. **Highest feasibility among goal-relevant candidates (secondary).** Restrict the set to candidates that act on the task's object (`targets_goal_object`), and choose the largest L among them. Because almost no goal-relevant candidate succeeds, rule 3 is reported only at states with at least one succeeding goal-relevant candidate (87 of the 728 even-step states). Elsewhere it must pick a failure whatever the scores are, so it would measure nothing. It is descriptive only.
4. **Small-menu rule 1, the "lure rate" (descriptive, amended).** At each even-step state whose proposal succeeds, the proposal is paired with one failing goal-object candidate at a time (5,475 pairs at 686 states), and rule 1 picks between the two; a lure is the failure picked. It isolates the goal-object pull from the composition of the full menu.

## Measures

For a model, a rule and a set variant, let $F_c$ be the share of even-step states whose chosen action fails under condition $c$. The primary contrast is aligned against neutral:

$$
\Delta F = F_{\text{aligned}} - F_{\text{neutral}} .
$$

Reported for each model, rule and set variant:

- the chosen-failure rate under each condition, and the difference above with its interval and permutation p;
- the share of chosen failures that are determined by the history;
- the **change rate**: the share of states where the chosen action differs between the two conditions, split into outcome-changing changes (one choice fails and the other succeeds) and changes between two failures or between two successes (a change from one working action to another is not harm);
- the **harm rate** (amended): the share of all states where the aligned choice fails and the neutral choice succeeds (task-induced harm), and likewise the help rate; among changed states, the shares of harm, help, both fail and both succeed. Over all states, the harm rate minus the help rate equals the difference above exactly; the analysis checks this identity;
- the share of choices that are the agent's proposal under each condition, and the failure rate of the choice by source (the proposal or a generated candidate) (amended);
- for rule 2, the difference split by acceptance cell (amended): states where the proposal is accepted under both tasks, rejected under both, accepted only under the aligned task, or accepted only under the neutral one, each with its count and its contribution to the difference (the contributions add up to it);
- for rule 2, separately: the **false-acceptance rate** (share of states with a failing proposal where the proposal is accepted; reported at even steps, odd steps and pooled) and the **false-rejection rate** (share of states with a succeeding proposal where it is rejected; even steps), under each condition, with the aligned-minus-neutral differences;
- calibration readouts (amended): at each even-step state, the neutral margin, the best succeeding candidate's rounded score minus the best failing candidate's under the neutral task, and the aligned shift on that best failure (its aligned minus neutral score), summarised by quartiles, the share of states whose margin is not positive, and the share of positive-margin states where the shift alone would close the margin.

**Baseline judgement, separated from the task-induced change.** A model can choose failures often under every task because it is poorly calibrated (Mistral already answers Yes to 45% of failures under the swapped task). So every rate is reported under the neutral task first, as the baseline, and the task-induced effect is always the aligned-minus-neutral difference, never the aligned rate alone. For each model and condition the analysis also reports the per-candidate accuracy (the share of candidates where the sign of L matches the true outcome), the share of failing candidates answered Yes, the share of succeeding candidates answered Yes, and two reference points: the failure rate of a uniformly random choice (the mean share of failures in the sets) and of an oracle (zero). The Yes rates and mean scores are also split by source (the agent's proposal or a generated candidate) by goal relevance by outcome, per condition and as aligned-minus-neutral and aligned-minus-swapped shifts (amended), so a change in decisions can be traced to the candidates whose scores moved.

The swapped condition gives the same measures for aligned against swapped and for swapped against neutral, on the states where it is defined.

**Decomposition (amended).** On the 676 even-step states with a swap, for every rule, the aligned-minus-neutral difference in chosen-failure rates splits exactly into an aligned-minus-swapped part (what naming the real task's object adds over a task of the same form about another object) and a swapped-minus-neutral part (what showing a task of that form does at all):

$$
F_{\text{aligned}} - F_{\text{neutral}} = (F_{\text{aligned}} - F_{\text{swapped}}) + (F_{\text{swapped}} - F_{\text{neutral}}) .
$$

All three are computed on the same states, so the identity is exact; the analysis checks it. From Stage 1a the same split is made against the task-blind verifier, on the same states:

$$
F_{\text{aligned}} - F_{\text{blind}} = (F_{\text{aligned}} - F_{\text{swapped}}) + (F_{\text{swapped}} - F_{\text{blind}}) .
$$

**Integer counts (review 2).** Each contrast also reports the harm and help counts and their difference, the net number of states the change moves to failure (harm minus help, so ΔF times the number of states); rule 2's contrasts add these per acceptance cell and the headroom in states (states where the proposal fails and the oracle's choice does not). The Option C rule compares these counts, not rates.

**Task-blind contrasts (Stage 1a).** For every rule the analysis reports, with the same measures as above, aligned against task-blind (the primary comparison for the question "is a task-aware verifier worse than a task-blind one?"), alongside aligned against neutral and aligned against swapped; swapped against task-blind and swapped against neutral (the decompositions' other parts); and neutral against task-blind, which shows whether "explore the room" behaves like no task. From review 2 every task-blind readout is reported against both task-blind prompts (`yn_blind` and, for the primary models, `yn_blind_all`), with the two compared directly, and rule 2's aligned-minus-task-blind difference is split, descriptively, into step 0 (134 even-step states, where no proposal fails) and later even steps (594 states). The per-candidate baseline, the source-by-goal shifts and the calibration readouts (margin under the task-blind verifier, aligned shift on its best failure) are reported for the task-blind arm too.

**Reference points: no verifier and a simulator oracle (Stage 1a, analysis only).** Every rule's failure rate is also read against what it would be without a world model and with a perfect one, on the even-step states (the swapped arm on those with a swap):

- **No verifier.** For rule 2, the agent's own proposal executed as is (its failure rate is the share of even-step states whose proposal fails). For rules 1 and 3, a uniformly random choice from the rule's set (each state counts its share of failures; this rate was already reported in each contrast as the random-choice rate and now gets an interval).
- **Simulator oracle.** A verifier that knows the true outcomes, fed to the same rules (score +1 for a candidate that succeeds, −1 for one that fails): rule 2 accepts exactly the succeeding proposals and otherwise falls back to a true success; rule 1 picks a true success. Every eligible state has a success in each set, so its failure rate is zero for every rule; it is computed rather than assumed.
- **Headroom:** the no-verifier rate minus the oracle rate, which is how much feasibility checking could reduce failures at all.
- **Each verifier against both**, per arm and rule: its failure rate, its failure rate minus the no-verifier one paired by state (game bootstrap interval and game permutation p; below zero, the verifier helps), minus the oracle one, and the share of the headroom it realises (the no-verifier rate minus its rate, divided by the headroom).
- **Rule 2 with one half perfect**, per arm: perfect accept/reject with the model's own fallback (failures come only from the fallback after a failing proposal is rejected), and the model's accept/reject with a perfect fallback (failures come only from accepting a failing proposal).

These are fixed by the candidate sets, not by any score, and were computed on the archived collection before any score: the proposal fails at 42 of the 728 even-step states (5.8%, game interval 3.7% to 7.9%), so rule 2's headroom is 5.8 points; a random choice fails at 68.1% of states for rule 1 with the proposal, 73.4% for rule 1 on the generated-only sets and 87.7% for rule 3 (at its 87 states). The reference points do not enter the Option C rule.

## Inference

Intervals are game-clustered percentile bootstraps (2,000 resamples of games, seed 20260924) of the per-state values. The p-value of a difference is a two-sided sign-flip permutation of the per-state paired differences (aligned minus neutral, each in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$) in which all states of a game flip together (1,000 permutations, seed 20260924), the test used throughout the study. States from one game share an episode, so the game is the unit of resampling. A percentile bootstrap of sparse differences in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ can be narrow (when few states change, many resamples look alike), so the Option C rule asks for the permutation p as well as the interval. No multiplicity adjustment is made; apart from the Option C rule below, every number is descriptive.

## Predictions (logged before any score)

1. Under rule 1 with the proposal, the aligned task raises the chosen-failure rate over the neutral task, $\Delta F > 0$, for both primary models. Mechanism (reworded after review 1): two components. An object-naming bias, by which the aligned task raises Yes on actions that act on the task's object, nearly all of which fail here; and a task-relevance penalty, by which a stated task lowers Yes on actions irrelevant to it, which includes most successes (unrelated `go to` actions). The first should show in the aligned-minus-swapped part of the decomposition, the second in the swapped-minus-neutral part.
2. (Revised after review 1.) Only 6 failing proposals act on the goal object; the goal-object mechanism predicts little change in false acceptance; this rate is reported with an interval and has very low power.
3. The size of both follows calibration rather than the size of the effect in nats. Llama-3.1-8B, which answers Yes to 21% of determined failures under the aligned task, will change more decisions than Qwen3-8B, which answers Yes to 0.2%; Qwen's change rate under rule 1 may be below 5%. Mistral-7B will change many decisions but from a high baseline failure rate.
4. Because the aligned task also raises Yes on working actions (by 2.44 nats for Qwen and 0.98 for Llama on the determined successes), it will lower rule 2's false-rejection rate of succeeding proposals, a task-induced help that partly offsets prediction 2.

## What each outcome implies for Option C

Option C would branch from real agent runs: at states along real episodes, replace the agent's next action with the planner's choice under each world-model condition and continue the episode, to measure the end-to-end cost. It is expensive (about 30 GPU-minutes per game, from RQ3), so it should run only if the bias reaches decisions often enough to matter. The rule, as amended after review 1, in Stage 1a and after review 2, each time before any score (the earlier versions are quoted in the amendment sections):

**Option C is worth doing if, for at least one primary model (Qwen3-8B, Llama-3.1-8B), on the 728 even-step states, under the decision rule Option C would itself run (rule 2: verification of the agent's own proposal, with fallback), comparing the aligned verifier with the task-blind one: ΔF = F(aligned) − F(task-blind) has a 95% game-bootstrap interval above zero and a game-permutation p below 0.05; the net number of states the aligned task moves to failure (harm minus help, that is ΔF times the number of states) is at least half of rule 2's headroom in states (the states where the proposal fails and the oracle's choice does not: 21 of 42 on the census), compared as integer counts; the states where the proposal is rejected by both verifiers contribute at most half of ΔF (by_acceptance.rejected_both.contribution ≤ 0.5 × ΔF, compared as net counts of states); and, on the 676 even-step states with a swap, the aligned-minus-task-blind ΔF is positive and the aligned-minus-swapped ΔF is positive with a point estimate at least half the aligned-minus-task-blind one. Rule 1 on the full sets is reported but cannot by itself trigger Option C: its ΔF follows from the item-level effect and the generator's menu. The harm rate, the help rate and a change rate (split into outcome-changing and success-to-success changes) are reported. The same rule with the neutral task in place of the task-blind verifier is computed for every primary model and reported as a secondary verdict, and the same rule against the task-blind prompt with the examples' task lines stripped too (`yn_blind_all`) as a prompt-format sensitivity verdict; neither triggers or blocks Option C.**

The aligned-minus-swapped and aligned-minus-task-blind differences of the last condition are both taken from the decomposition against the task-blind verifier, on the even-step states with a swap, so they are on the same states (for the other verdicts, from the decomposition against their own comparison). Each primary model falls in exactly one of these categories, checked in this order:

1. **Worth doing:** every condition above holds.
2. **Toward failure, through the fallback menu:** every condition holds except that the states where both verifiers reject the proposal contribute more than half of ΔF. There rule 2 is rule 1 on the generated-only menu, which review 1 kept out of the gate because its difference follows from the menu.
3. **Toward failure, not attributable to the real task:** the interval is above zero, p is below 0.05 and the net count reaches half the headroom, but on the states with a swap the aligned-minus-task-blind difference is not positive, or the aligned-minus-swapped difference is not positive or is less than half of it. Stating a task moves decisions toward failure, but a task about another object does it nearly as much (whatever the fallback share).
4. **Toward failure, too rare:** the interval is above zero and p is below 0.05, but the net count is below half the headroom.
5. **Changes toward success:** the 95% interval of ΔF is entirely below zero: the aligned task makes rule 2 choose failures less often than the task-blind verifier.
6. **No reliable change in failures:** none of the above (the interval reaches zero, or it is above zero but p is at least 0.05).

The overall verdict waits until both primary models are scored with all their arms; it is then the first category in the order above that some primary model reaches, and only "worth doing" triggers Option C. The secondary verdict against the neutral task and the prompt-format verdict against `yn_blind_all` are formed the same way and reported next to it; where either disagrees, the report says so and the task-blind verdict stands (a difference from the `yn_blind_all` verdict is reported as sensitivity to the prompt format). A secondary model never decides it; one whose rule 2 moves to failure (for example Mistral) would be reported as the case where the bias does reach decisions. Rule 1, rule 3, the generated-only sets, the lure rate, the step split and the calibration readouts inform the reading but do not enter the rule.

**What can and cannot reach the threshold (stated before data, review 2).** Harm counts states where the aligned choice fails and the task-blind choice succeeds, so harm can never exceed the aligned verifier's own failures. At a state whose proposal works, the aligned choice fails only if the aligned verifier rejects the working proposal (a false rejection) and its fallback fails. So the net count is at most the number of working proposals the aligned verifier rejects with a failing fallback, plus the number of the 42 failing-proposal states at which the aligned choice fails. A low aligned false-rejection rate therefore confines a "worth doing" to the 42 failing-proposal states, where the aligned verifier would have to fail at least 21 more of them than the task-blind one; with few false rejections and a false-acceptance difference well below 21 states, "worth doing" is out of reach whatever the task does. (Review 2 had worded this as "a low aligned false-rejection rate rules worth doing out"; the failing-proposal route is the exception, so it is stated as above.) The Stage 1a reading aid that compared a 5% harm rate with the 5.8-point headroom was wrong and is withdrawn: harm is gross and mostly arises at working proposals, whereas the headroom counts failing ones.

**A verifier that does not pay for itself (stated before data, review 2).** If the task-blind verifier already fails more often under rule 2 than executing the proposal as is (5.8% of even-step states), it does not pay for itself on these menus, and any "worth doing" is harm inside a verifier that should not be deployed here. The category is not changed, but the report flags it (`worth_doing_inside_a_verifier_that_does_not_pay`) and the result is to be read as a statement about that verifier, not as a reason to fear task-aware verification in general.

**What each outcome would license (review 2).**

- **Worth doing:** on these menus, a verifier that sees the task makes the agent's own verified plan fail on at least half as many net states as verification could ever fix, through its judgement of the proposal rather than through the fallback menu, and it does so because the real task's object is named. That licenses running Option C for that model, and a decision-level (not end-to-end) claim that task-aware verification can hurt. It does not license a claim about task success, about other menus or agents, or (if flagged) about a verifier that pays for itself.
- **Toward failure, through the fallback menu:** the difference is reliable and large enough, but it arises mostly where both verifiers reject the proposal, where the choice is rule 1 on the generated menu (goal-object failures against unrelated `go to` actions). It licenses a statement about how the task changes the ranking of that menu, not about verification; Option C is not run, and the question moves to a stage with different menus.
- **Toward failure, not attributable to the real task:** stating a task of this form moves decisions toward failure, but a task about another object does it about as much. It licenses "showing a task hurts this verifier", not "naming the goal object does"; Option C would not isolate the goal-object bias.
- **Toward failure, too rare:** the change is reliable but nets fewer than half the states verification could fix. It licenses "the bias reaches decisions, rarely, in this setting"; Option C is not worth its cost.
- **Changes toward success:** the task makes rule 2 fail less often. It licenses the statement that a task-aware verifier does better here, which weighs against the harm story at the decision level.
- **No reliable change in failures:** no change was detected. This is not evidence of no effect: with 728 states clustered in 134 games, a difference of roughly 2 to 3 points in the failure rate (about 15 to 20 net states) or less could go undetected.

## The scoring run (not yet run)

`decisionsim_score_<model>`, one run per model, all 761 eligible states and all four arms (aligned, neutral, swapped, task-blind), plus `yn_blind_all` for the two primary models (review 2), sharded by game (`WF_SHARD`, `WF_N_SHARDS`; one shard by default), yes/no only, using the existing scorer (`LogoddsScorer.score_one_token_pair`) at the pinned revisions in `wf.constants.REVISIONS`. Every condition of a state is scored in the same job, so each paired comparison is on one GPU. Each score is saved as it finishes, and a resume (`WF_RESUME=1`) skips saved scores and refuses saved scores that belong to different items or a different sample. Prompts are exactly `rq1v`'s (`sizes.arm_messages` for the reference models, `families.family_messages` for the family models, so Gemma's system text is folded into the first turn as in wave A); the task-blind prompt is the model's aligned prompt with the query's task sentence removed (`decisionsim.blind_messages`), `yn_blind_all` that prompt with the examples' task lines removed too (`decisionsim.strip_example_tasks`), and both go through the Yes/No gate with the others. The family models get a Yes/No tokenization gate before scoring, as in wave A.

Models: Qwen3-8B (thinking off) and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (primary); Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3, Gemma-2-9B-it and Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct (secondary). Llama-3.2-1B is left out: it failed wave A's competence gate. Scoring order (review 2): the two primary models first, since the verdict needs only them; the three secondary models after, all five kept.

**Cost (amended for the census, for Stage 1a and after review 2).** Wave A's family runs took 103 to 107 minutes on an A40 or L40S for about 43,700 forward passes of 7–9B models (about 0.15 s a pass) and 33 minutes on an A100 for Llama-3.2-3B (about 0.045 s a pass). The census has 9,930 candidates: aligned, neutral and task-blind for each and swapped for the 9,196 candidates at the 709 states with a swap, 38,986 single-token forward passes per secondary model, and 48,916 per primary model with `yn_blind_all` (9,930 more; checked by a dry run on the archived collection). That is about 125 minutes for each primary model, about 100 for Mistral and Gemma and about 30 for Llama-3.2-3B, loading included; all five models come to about 8 GPU-hours (7.5–8.5), of which about 4 are the primary models. The sbatch asks for 6.5 hours per job, about three times the longest estimate, so a slow node cannot cut a job off; a job that is cut off resumes from its saved scores.

## Caveats

- One agent (Qwen3-8B), one collection, the first 12 steps of each episode, one prompt format.
- The decision rules see only feasibility. A real planner weighs progress too; rule 3 is a gesture toward that and is underpowered.
- The candidate mix is set by the generators; the share of goal-object failures among candidates drives rule 1's failure rate under every condition.
- The neutral task is itself unusual for an agent's world model; the aligned-minus-neutral contrast measures what showing the task does, not what a different real task would do (the swapped arm does that).
- The task-blind prompt is unusual in one way: the few-shot examples' histories start with a task line and the query's history has none. That mismatch is part of what a task-blind verifier with this fixed prompt sees; `yn_blind_all` (primary models only) removes it. "Task-blind" means no stated task: the history after the first step still reflects an agent pursuing the task, identically in every arm.

## Amendments after review 1, before any score (2026-11-20)

Review 1 passed the engineering with fixes and failed the science as written. Every change below was made before any decision-simulation score existed (no `decisionsim_score_<model>` run had started), so none of them could have been chosen with knowledge of a result. The counts quoted were rechecked by a dry run of the amended code on the archived collection (`text-wm-fix17`), which builds the states and candidate sets but reads no model score.

1. **Odd-step states left out of state-level rates.** The collector ran the broad generator (whose candidates include the agent's proposal) at even steps always and at odd steps only when the proposal was invalid (`wf/data/transitions.py`, `build_transitions_from_rollout`, k = 2). The first version did not know this, so its eligible set mixed 728 even-step states with 33 odd-step states that are eligible only because the agent's action failed. That selection inflates every rate that involves the proposal (rule 2 especially, and the generated-only contrast is no cure, since the set's existence is what is selected). Every state-level rate is now on the 728 even-step states (constant `BROAD_EVERY_K = 2`); the odd-step states enter only the false-acceptance rate, which is conditional on a failing proposal, reported by parity and pooled.
2. **Scores rounded to 1/64 nat.** Scores are rounded to 1/64 nat (the bf16 resolution of the logit difference) before any comparison; ties are then exact. The existing yes/no scores sit on that grid up to float noise, so without rounding two candidates the model cannot tell apart could be ordered by noise, a tie could be missed, and a score of a millionth of a nat would count as Yes. Raw scores are kept for the mean-score readouts.
3. **A new Option C rule.** The original rule was: Option C is worth doing if, for at least one primary model, under rule 1 or rule 2 on the with-proposal sets, the change rate between the aligned and neutral tasks is at least 5% of states and ΔF is positive with a 95% interval that excludes zero; "weak case" if the change rate reached 5% without that; otherwise not worth doing. Three problems: (a) rule 1 on the full sets has a ΔF that follows almost mechanically from the item-level effect and the generator's menu (mostly goal-object failures against unrelated `go to` successes), so it could trigger Option C without saying anything new about planning; Option C would run rule 2, verification of the agent's own proposal, so the gate now uses rule 2. (b) A change rate counts changes from one working action to another, which cost nothing; the gate now uses the harm rate (aligned choice fails, neutral choice succeeds) and the change rate is reported split. (c) A negative ΔF with a 5% change rate was labelled "weak case", which misreads a change toward success; it now has its own category, and every category is defined. The gate also asks for the permutation p (the percentile bootstrap of sparse per-state differences of minus one, zero or one can be narrow) and for the swapped attribution check (next item). The rule is quoted in full in "What each outcome implies for Option C".
4. **Swapped task scored for every model.** The aligned-minus-neutral contrast mixes two things: what naming the real task's object does, and what showing any task of that form does (the neutral line has no object and no goal at all). The swapped arm separates them, so it is now scored for all five models, the decomposition aligned − neutral = (aligned − swapped) + (swapped − neutral) is reported on the even-step states with a swap, and the Option C rule asks that the aligned-minus-swapped part be positive and at least half of the whole. Prediction 1's mechanism was reworded to name both components (object-naming bias and a task-relevance penalty on irrelevant actions).
5. **Source and goal-relevance splits; prediction 2 revised.** Rule 2's behaviour depends on the agent's proposal, which is a different kind of candidate from the generated ones (the proposal is mostly a working `go to`; the generated failures mostly act on the goal object). The per-candidate Yes rates and mean scores are now split by source by goal relevance by outcome, per condition and as shifts, and each contrast reports how often the choice is the proposal and how often a chosen proposal or a chosen generated candidate fails. Prediction 2 was revised before any data: of the 75 failing proposals only 6 act on the goal object (4 at even steps), so the goal-object mechanism predicts little change in false acceptance, and the rate has very low power.
6. **Tests that each condition gets its own prompt.** A test now records every prompt the scoring run sends and checks the task line per arm (the real line; "Your task is to: explore the room."; the line from `make_swapped_task_line` with the state's swap object), the yes/no question at the end, and that each candidate is scored once per arm. Another checks that a state without a swap object is kept when the swapped arm is scored.
7. **Tie order.** Ties now go to a seeded per-state hash of the action instead of alphabetical order, which favoured the same verbs at every state (and `examine` and `go to`, mostly successes, over `take`, mostly failures). The analysis also counts tied top sets whose candidates differ in outcome and reports a sensitivity readout that splits each tied set evenly.
8. **Rule 2 by acceptance cell; calibration readouts; lure rate.** Rule 2's difference is split by whether the proposal was accepted under both tasks, neither, or one of them, so a change in the fallback can be told from a change in verification. Each even-step state's neutral margin and the aligned shift on its best failure show how close rule 1 is to a flip and how far the task moves it. The small-menu lure rate pairs a working proposal with one goal-object failure at a time.
9. **Census.** All 761 eligible states are scored instead of a 300-state sample, with the swapped arm: 29,056 passes per model, about 73 minutes for an 8B model, 4.5–5 GPU-hours for all five; the sbatch limit is 4 hours per job.
10. **Facts added to the text.** The last saved step of each game is excluded (134 states: 19 at even steps, 115 at odd steps); 666 of the 761 eligible states have exactly 4 succeeding candidates; the percentile bootstrap on sparse differences can be narrow, hence the permutation p in the gate.


## Stage 1a additions, before any score (2026-11-20)

The study now runs in stages. This stage (1a) keeps the fixed candidate menus and measures how vulnerable a verifier that consults the world model is to the task-induced bias; later stages would change the menus or run the agent. Three additions were made, all before any decision-simulation score existed (no `decisionsim_score_<model>` run had started), so none of them could have been chosen with knowledge of a result. The counts below come from a dry run of the code on the archived collection (`text-wm-fix17`), which builds the states, candidate sets and prompts but reads no model score.

1. **A task-blind verifier arm (`yn_blind`).** The same yes/no prompt with the task removed, not replaced. *Where the task is.* The environment server keeps ALFWorld's first observation whole (`wf/env/server.py`; the history starts with it, `wf/env/replay.py`), and that observation is the room description, a line break, and the task sentence "Your task is to: ...". The dry run checked all 9,930 candidates of the census: in every history the task sentence is the second line, it is exactly the candidate's task line, it comes before the first action, and it occurs once. *What is removed.* That sentence and the line break before it, so no empty line is left; every other byte of the prompt stays (`decisionsim.blind_history`, `decisionsim.blind_messages`; the code refuses a history with no task sentence, two, one outside the first observation, or one that is not the real task line). For all five models' prompt builders the dry run confirmed that each blind prompt equals the aligned prompt with exactly that substring deleted, and that every message before the query (system text and examples) is identical. *The few-shot examples keep their task lines.* They are part of the fixed prompt shared by every arm; they name other games' tasks (a soapbottle in the toilet, a candle on a cabinet, two soapbottles in the garbage can), which say nothing about the query's task; removing them would change the examples as well as the query, so the blind-minus-aligned contrast would no longer isolate the query's task sentence; and every earlier run (RQ2, `rq1v`, the families) used them unchanged. The cost of that choice is a format mismatch (the examples' histories open with a task line and the blind query's does not), listed under "Caveats". *What it adds.* 9,930 passes per model (one per candidate), from 29,056 to 38,986; the five configs and `DEFAULT_ARMS` include it. *What is reported.* Every rule and measure for aligned against task-blind (the primary comparison for whether a task-aware verifier is worse than a task-blind one), alongside aligned against neutral and against swapped; swapped against task-blind; neutral against task-blind; and the decomposition aligned − task-blind = (aligned − swapped) + (swapped − task-blind), on the even-step states with a swap.

2. **The Option C rule uses the task-blind verifier as its comparison; the neutral version is secondary.** The rule's text is unchanged except that "neutral" becomes "task-blind" throughout (harm: the aligned choice fails and the task-blind choice succeeds; ΔF = F(aligned) − F(task-blind); the aligned-minus-swapped part at least half the aligned-minus-task-blind one, from the decomposition against the task-blind verifier). The same rule against the neutral task is computed and reported as a secondary verdict that never decides. The amended rule is quoted in full in "What each outcome implies for Option C". Reasons: (a) Option C asks whether showing the task to the verifier harms a planner enough to be worth an end-to-end test; if it does, the remedy a builder would deploy is to leave the task out of the verifier's prompt, not to substitute an invented one, so aligned against task-blind is the contrast whose answer says what to do. (Reworded after review 2: what this arm shows is the query without its task, inside the fixed few-shot prompt whose examples keep their task lines; a builder deploying a task-blind verifier would likely remove those too, which this arm does not; `yn_blind_all` does, for the primary models.) (b) "Explore the room" is itself a task, one the agent never has; whatever it does (it might, for example, raise Yes on `go to` actions, most of which succeed here) enters the aligned-minus-neutral contrast and would be credited to the real task. The task-blind prompt states no task, so aligned minus task-blind is the whole effect of stating the task. (c) The neutral task was the control of RQ2, of the yes/no replication and of this rule before Stage 1a; keeping it as the secondary verdict keeps the link to those results and shows whether the decision depends on the choice of control, and the neutral-against-task-blind contrast shows where a disagreement comes from. The swap-share attribution check keeps its role: it asks whether naming the real task's object, not stating a task of that form, does the work. Before Stage 1a the rule read: "the task-induced harm rate (aligned choice fails, neutral choice succeeds) is at least 5% of states, ΔF has a 95% game-bootstrap interval above zero and a game-permutation p below 0.05, and the aligned-minus-swapped ΔF under the same rule is positive with a point estimate at least half the aligned-minus-neutral one", with ΔF = F(aligned) − F(neutral) and otherwise the same text. The overall verdict now waits for both primary models to be scored with all four arms.

3. **No-verifier and simulator-oracle reference points (analysis only).** Specified under "Reference points" in "Measures". For rule 2, no verifier means executing the agent's proposal as is; for rules 1 and 3, a uniformly random choice from the rule's set (this random-choice rate already existed in every contrast; it now gets an interval and a paired comparison). The oracle is a verifier with the true outcomes (rule 2 accepts exactly the succeeding proposals and falls back to a true success; rule 1 picks a true success), plus two half-oracles for rule 2 (perfect accept/reject with the model's fallback; the model's accept/reject with a perfect fallback). Their purpose is to show whether feasibility checking could help at all here: with no verifier the proposal fails at 42 of 728 even-step states (5.8%), so verification can remove at most 5.8 points of failures, while it can reject any of the 686 succeeding proposals; a random choice fails at 68.1% (rule 1 with the proposal), 73.4% (generated only) and 87.7% (rule 3, 87 states). These numbers depend only on the candidate sets. Nothing here enters the Option C rule.

**Cost and limits.** 38,986 passes per model: about 100 minutes for a 7–9B model and about 30 for Llama-3.2-3B, about 7 GPU-hours for all five (from 4.5–5). The sbatch limit goes from 4 to 5 hours per job, about three times the estimate. A correction made at the same time: the cost paragraph said the swapped arm covers 7,196 candidates; it covers 9,196 (the total of 29,056 was right).

**Not changed.** The states, candidate sets, rounding, tie hash, the three rules, the even-step restriction, the inference, the predictions and the thresholds. No new prediction is logged for the task-blind arm.
